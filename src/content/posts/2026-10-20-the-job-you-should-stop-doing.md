You got this role because you were good at the job below it. That's usually how it works. You ran the best shift, closed the most cases, kept the tidiest records, and someone above you decided that meant you were ready to lead the people who used to be your peers.

Here is the part nobody tells you in the promotion conversation: the instinct that got you here will now work against you.

For years your value was measured by what you personally produced. A line running well because you were the one adjusting it. A customer problem solved because you were the one who picked up the phone. That feeling of being useful, of ending the day with something visibly fixed by your own hands, is hard to give up. So most new leaders don't give it up. They keep doing pieces of their old job on top of the new one, because it's the work they're fastest at and the work that gives them that hit of "I did something today."

Six months in, you'll recognize the pattern if you're honest with yourself. You're the one who still jumps in when a machine trips, while your new team stands back and watches you handle it, the way they always have. You're the one who still drafts the report yourself at 9 p.m. because it's faster than explaining what you want to someone else. Meanwhile the actual job you were promoted into, deciding what the team should focus on, coaching the person who's struggling, having the conversation you've been avoiding with someone who isn't pulling their weight, sits untouched, because it doesn't feel as immediately productive.

I did this for the better part of a year the first time I led a team. I was proud of how fast I could still solve a technical problem on the floor. What I didn't notice was that every time I solved it myself, I was teaching my team that the problem was mine to solve, not theirs. They stopped bringing me solutions and started bringing me the problem itself, because that had worked every time before.

The shift, when it finally happened for me, wasn't dramatic. It was a decision to answer a different question before doing anything: is this something only I can do, or something I'm doing because I'm faster at it than they are right now? The second category is the trap. Someone has to become slower at this before they can become faster than you eventually were. If you keep doing it, that someone never gets the chance.

Try this in your first month, if you're early enough that the habit hasn't set yet. Pick one thing you do well and hand it to someone on your team this week, badly at first, on purpose. Watch how uncomfortable it feels to let it go slower than you'd have done it. That discomfort is the actual job now. The technical work was the old one.

You will still be tempted, on your hardest days, to grab the wrench, the phone, the keyboard, and just handle it yourself. Some days that's the right call. But if it's your default by month three, you haven't become a leader yet. You've just become a very senior version of what you already were.
