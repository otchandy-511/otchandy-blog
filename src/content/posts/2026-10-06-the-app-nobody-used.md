A few years back I ran the rollout of a mobile app meant to replace paper checklists on a bottling line. The vendor had a good name, the demo looked clean, and I had sign-off from my own boss before we started. I remember thinking the hard part was behind us.

The app asked operators to log a handful of readings each shift: line speed, downtime reason, a photo if something looked off. Small ask, on paper. We handed out tablets, ran two training sessions, and told the shift leads to hold people accountable for using it.

Four months later, usage had dropped to almost nothing. A few operators still logged data most days. Most had quietly gone back to the clipboard hanging by the line, the same one we'd told them to retire.

I spent the first few weeks blaming the users. Not in those exact words, but that was the shape of my thinking. Adoption is a training problem, I told myself, so we ran another round of training. It didn't move the number.

What actually happened was simpler than I wanted it to be. The tablet was one more thing to carry on a shift where operators already had both hands full most of the time. Their own phone was in their pocket already. Typing on a tablet screen with gloved hands, standing next to a running line, took longer and felt worse than writing three words on a clipboard. I had asked people to make their job slightly harder, every single shift, on the promise that the data would help someone else, somewhere else, make better decisions later. That trade never once felt worth it from where they were standing.

Nobody told me this directly for the first two months. Shift leads reported the numbers were "improving," because reporting a problem with a project their boss cared about is its own kind of risk. I only found out what was really going on when I stood on the floor for three shifts in a row instead of reading the dashboard from my office.

Here is what I'd do differently, and what I now do differently. Before I ask a team to change how they work, I try to answer one question honestly: what does this cost the person doing it, in the moment, on their worst day? Not what it costs the organization, not what it saves six months from now. What it costs the operator at 2 a.m. with a jammed line and a supervisor breathing down their neck. If the honest answer is "more effort than what they had before," the tool will die no matter how good the training is.

The fix, when we eventually found one, wasn't a better app. It was cutting the ask down to almost nothing: one number, entered on a device already in their hand, only when something was actually wrong. Less data. Better data, because it was the only version anyone was willing to actually give us.

If you're introducing anything new to a team you lead, spend a shift doing the job yourself with the new tool before you roll it out. Not watching someone else do it. Doing it. If you flinch at the extra step, they will too.
